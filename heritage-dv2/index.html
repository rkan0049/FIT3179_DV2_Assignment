Listed, but is it safe?

Australia records and recognises remarkable places, from ancient rock shelters to convict prisons and coral reefs. But does being recognised mean being protected? This story follows that question through six chapters: what is on the list, where it is, how it has grown, whether it is safe, what threatens it, and who pays to look after it.

Listing counts, survey answers and funding totals each measure something different, so every chart explains what its numbers can and cannot tell us. Hover over any chart for details, or use the menu above to jump to a chapter.

01

What is on the list?

126places on the National Heritage List

The National Heritage List started in 2004. It is reserved for places with “outstanding heritage value to the nation”, and every place is listed as one of three types: natural (landscapes, reefs, fossils and wildlife), historic (buildings, sites and events that shaped the nation) or Indigenous (places important to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples).

232,000 Indigenous sites recorded
15,000 Historic places on state registers
8,800 Underwater sites
389 Commonwealth places
126 National Heritage places
21 World Heritage properties

Heritage in Australia works like a pyramid. At the bottom are hundreds of thousands of sites recorded by the states. Near the top are the 126 National Heritage places, which the Australian Government is responsible for, and above them the 21 World Heritage properties. This page focuses on the National Heritage List: their national significance makes them an important test of whether recognition leads to meaningful protection.

The National Heritage family tree

The List branches into its three heritage types, then into states and territories, then into all 126 places. More than half are historic places, and only 20 are listed for their Indigenous values, even though Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultures go back more than 65,000 years. Hover over a branch to highlight its heritage type, and click a place to open its official page.

What to noticeThe Indigenous branch is the thinnest: 20 of 126 places, for cultures more than 65,000 years old.

Source: DCCEEW, National Heritage List Spatial Database

Where is it?

119National Heritage places shown on the map of the mainland and Tasmania

National Heritage places are not spread evenly. Most sit in the south-east, while some jurisdictions have only a handful of listings. Hover for counts, then click a state to compare it with the rest.

Every National Heritage place in Australia

Hover over a dot for details, click it to open its official page, or click a legend item to highlight one heritage type.

Source: DCCEEW, National Heritage List Spatial Database

Each dot is a National Heritage place. This map shows 119 of the 126. The other seven are in external territories or on Macquarie Island.

Zoom in on the south-east and the map fills up. 73 places, six in every ten, sit in this corner of the country, and 56 of them are historic: convict sites, colonial buildings and places tied to early settlement around Sydney, Melbourne and Hobart.

Now look north of the Tropic of Capricorn. Across the whole tropical north there are only 14 places. Nine are natural, like Kakadu and the Great Barrier Reef, and five are Indigenous. Not one is historic.

Only 20 places are listed for their Indigenous values, spread thinly across every state, even though Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have cared for this whole continent for more than 65,000 years.

A map tells us where. Time tells us when.

The distribution is uneven today, but was it built gradually? Listing dates and state-register additions show how recognition has expanded, and where the pace differs.

03

How has protection expanded?

75places listed within the first four years

Protection grows in three ways: new places join the National Heritage List, states add places to their own registers, and new sites are recorded on land and underwater. But is it growing fast enough, and evenly?

National Heritage listings over time

75 of the 126 places (60%) were listed in the first four years, including 16 World Heritage places added on a single day in 2007. Since 2008, only about three places have been added each year. Drag across the lower chart to zoom in.

Source: DCCEEW, National Heritage List Spatial Database

Historic places added to state registers each year

Over five years (2015–16 to 2019–20), states added 402 historic places to their registers, but the effort is uneven: Queensland added 90, while Tasmania added only 6. Circle area shows the number of places. Use the dropdown to see each financial year.

Source: Australia State of the Environment 2021, heritage data

On land or underwater? Heritage added to registers, 2016–17 to 2019–20

This chart covers a shorter window, the four years from 2016–17 to 2019–20, because no underwater data was reported for 2015–16. In those four years, 428 underwater sites were added, compared with 291 historic places on land. New South Wales alone added 224 underwater sites, more than half the national total, while Western Australia added none despite its long coastline. The ACT has no coastline, and the Australian Government manages underwater sites in Commonwealth waters rather than a historic places register.

Source: Australia State of the Environment 2021, heritage data

What gets recorded? Indigenous heritage sites added to Queensland’s database, 2012–2020

Queensland recorded 15,164 new Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander heritage sites in nine years, and three in four are artefact scatters (stone tools and flakes left on the ground). Pick a region to see how recording differs across the state. Queensland only. Darker cells mean more sites recorded that year. The colour scale is compressed so that smaller numbers are still visible.

Source: Queensland State of the Environment 2020, cultural heritage site locations

More listings do not automatically mean better protection.

Counting newly recognised places cannot tell us whether they are properly managed. To test the promise of listing, we turn to laws, stakeholder experiences and management plans.

04

Are these places actually safe?

73 of 120places with plans rated effective in the 2023 review

A listing is a promise, not a guarantee. It only helps if there are laws that work, a plan to manage the place, and people with the power to look after it. Here is what the people closest to these places say.

Do Indigenous stakeholders think heritage is protected by law?

Each square is one in every 100 respondents. Almost all of them said Australian laws do not adequately protect Indigenous heritage. This was a survey of Indigenous stakeholders for the 2021 State of the Environment report, not of all Indigenous Australians.

Source: Australia State of the Environment 2021, Indigenous stakeholder survey

What Indigenous stakeholders see as the biggest pressures

Protecting cultural sites (72%) and protecting Indigenous knowledge (69%) top the list, well ahead of climate change (43%). Respondents could choose more than one option, so the totals add up to more than 100%.

Source: Australia State of the Environment 2021, Indigenous stakeholder survey

Does national recognition match what states are still adding?

Each state is coloured by two measures at once, both per million residents: National Heritage places (June 2020) and historic places added to its state register from 2016–17 to 2019–20. Each measure is split into low and high at the middle value of the eight states and territories (5.5 national places and 12.3 new additions per million). Western Australia, the Northern Territory and the ACT are high on both, while New South Wales, Victoria and South Australia are low on both. Hover over a state for its figures, or click a legend square to highlight its states.

What to noticeThis shows listing activity, not how well places are protected. The two lists also cover different things, so a state can be low on one and still look after its heritage well.

Sources: DCCEEW, National Heritage List (listings to June 2020); Australia State of the Environment 2021, historic places added to registers; ABS population, June 2020

Listed, but is anyone managing it? Management plans for the 120 National Heritage places, 2023

Only 73 of the 120 places (61%) have a management plan that their own managers rate as effective. For 35 places, there is either no plan or no answer at all. Each bar shows how many places are left at that step, and the gold notes show how many drop out. Effectiveness is self-reported by place managers. The department later confirmed that 6 of the 17 places without a plan have one in place or in preparation. “Effectiveness not rated” is calculated from the report text (85 plans minus 77 ratings).

Source: DCCEEW (2023), Review of the National Heritage List and Commonwealth Heritage List, Appendix F

Protection on paper meets pressures on the ground.

Even when a place has legal recognition and a management plan, climate, development and inadequate resourcing can still threaten it. The next evidence comes from heritage experts.

05

What is threatening them?

41%of natural-heritage pressure mentions attributed to climate change

For the 2021 State of the Environment report, heritage experts were asked which pressures have the greatest impact on each type of heritage. Their answers fall into five groups: climate change, industry, population, governance and other. Then we check what UNESCO itself has flagged for Australia’s World Heritage places.

What do experts say is hurting heritage most?

For natural heritage, climate change is the biggest worry (41%). But for geoheritage, Indigenous and historic heritage, the top group is governance: too little money and weak legal protection. The single most named pressure overall is inadequate resourcing. Column width shows how many mentions each heritage type received. Click any tile to see the pressures behind it.

What to noticeClimate change leads only for natural heritage. For every other type, the bigger problem is governance: not enough money and weak laws.

Source: McConnell (2021a), heritage expert survey, Australia State of the Environment 2021

Even World Heritage is on watch: UNESCO reports on Australian properties, 1983–2026

When UNESCO is worried about a World Heritage property, it asks for a State of Conservation report. Since 1983, 14 of Australia’s 21 World Heritage properties have been reported on 88 times. The Great Barrier Reef and the Tasmanian Wilderness lead with 20 reports each, and Kakadu follows with 12, mostly about mining. Across all reports, mining is the most raised threat (36 reports), followed by weak management (34). Climate change and fire came up only twice before 2011, but in 11 of the 16 years since. Spike height shows the number of reports. Pick a threat from the dropdown to see which properties UNESCO raised it for, and click a spike to see that property’s threats in the timeline (click an empty part of the map to reset). Macquarie Island (4 reports) and Heard and McDonald Islands (3) are too far south to fit on the map but are included in the timeline. Threats are grouped from UNESCO’s 30 threat categories.

What to noticeThese are the best protected places in the country, yet the Reef and the Tasmanian Wilderness have each been reported on 20 times.

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, State of Conservation database (downloaded 25 October 2026)

Threats raise a practical question: who pays to respond?

After identifying the risks, we follow the money: where Australian heritage grants go, and how concentrated World Heritage funding is.

06

Can conservation keep up?

Looking after heritage costs money: for repairs, for management plans and for research. The Australian Heritage Grants program funds projects at National Heritage places, and Round 8 (2025–26) shows where that money went.

Where the money goes: Australian Heritage Grants, Round 8 (2025–26)

Round 8 funded 20 projects worth $5.2 million. Historic places received 55% and natural places 42%, but only one project ($0.12 million, about 2%) went to a place listed for its Indigenous values. Some projects at natural places, such as Willandra Lakes, are Indigenous-led, so this shows how places are listed rather than every dollar that helps Indigenous heritage. Projects are grouped by the main purpose in their description. Amounts exclude GST. Hover over a flow for details.

Source: DCCEEW, Australian Heritage Grants Round 8 successful projects

The big picture: who gets the money to manage World Heritage?

Between 2011–12 and 2015–16, the Australian Government spent $485 million managing World Heritage properties. Just three of them, the Great Barrier Reef, Kakadu and Uluru-Kata Tjuta, received 81%. Ningaloo Coast received $3.2 million (0.7%). Bars show each property’s funding, largest first, and the line shows the running total. Hover for details, or pick a single financial year. Heard and McDonald Islands received no funding in this period, so it is not shown. Amounts in millions of dollars; this is older data than the rest of the page.

Source: Department of the Environment and Energy, World Heritage management funding 2011–12 to 2015–16

Australia has listed 126 places of outstanding value to the nation, and hundreds of thousands more sit on state registers. But the data tells us that a listing is only the start. Most national listings happened in the first few years. Almost every Indigenous stakeholder surveyed says the law does not protect their heritage. Almost a third of National Heritage places cannot show an effective management plan, and experts say the biggest gap is resourcing. Even World Heritage places like the Reef and Ningaloo are now under growing climate pressure.

Listed is not the same as safe.
